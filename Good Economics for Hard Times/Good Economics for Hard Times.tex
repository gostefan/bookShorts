\section{MEGA: Make Economics Great Again}
\begin{mdframed}[style=mystyle,frametitle=Core Message]
Economists should not to just share their conclusions but also the thinking path to it.
\end{mdframed}

General standpoints/ideologies of people (e.g. gender roles) are better predictors of their policiy views than statistical data (e.g. income, home town, demographic group) \cite{hidden-tribes}. The most central topics are Immigration, trade, taxes, and the role of government.

The broad population often disagrees with Economists \cite{eco-vs-american}. Even informing people about prominent economist's views doesn't change their opinion.

Media often calls on the loudest economists. Sadly these are typically not the best but still drive public discourse.

Economy is still a combination of intuition based in science, some guesswork aided by experience, and a bunch of pure trial and error. That means economists often get things wrong.

All of these reasons lead to people not trusting economists. The only approach that helps here is if Economists explained their conclusions - The path there is at least equally important.

In this book we shouldn't forget humans want more than money and consumer goods. They want a good life. This includes dignity, family, respect, lightness, pleasure, etc.

\section{From the Mouth of the Shark}
People have a wrong perception of migrants. They think there are more of them than there actually are, that immigrants are less educated, poorer more likely to be unemployed and welfare dependent than they actually are.

The voting behavior doesn't change with fact checking politician's wrong claims.

The logic that the labour market behaves like the free market is nice and easy. More labor supply means lower wages. But this fact is wrong as we will see.

There is no evidence that even large influx of low-skilled immigrants hurt the local population.

\subsection{Leaving Home}
Immigrants don't come from the poorest countries in the world. People leave because they find it intolerable because everyday normality collapses.

In years with bad harvests less people migrate because they can't afford it \cite{push-and-pull}.
Bad years mean less people move because they can't afford it.

Most people don't move just because they can earn more. (There are exceptions as always.) Neither do Indians move from the countryside to Delhi (which would double their income) nor did the Greeks emigrate massively while unemployment was at 27 percent in 2013-2014 \cite{greek-emigration}.

\subsection{The Migration Lottery}
With migration we focus on the wages of migrants and not on the reasons why they move. The \keyword{identification problem} describes that you cannot compare the migrants with the non-migrants from the same location. The migrants may take more risks, have more stamina, or other skills that would have made them successful even without migrating.

To claim the difference in wages is caused by the difference in location and nothing else a connection between the cause and the effect have to be established without tainting factors.

Visa lotteries allow for such comparison. In New Zealand it was found that migrants from Tonga tripled their wage \cite{new-zealand-lottery}. Indian software professionals make six times more money in the United States \cite{usa-lottery}.

\subsection{Lava Bombs}
A volcanic eruption in Iceland destroyed some buildings in a fishing village while other survived. It was possible to assume that nothing distinguished the people affected.

Young people moving (typically with their parents) earned about \$3'000 more than those staying. This is mostly because they likely attended college and didn't become fishermen \cite{iceland-volcano}.

After WWII in Finland a large number of Finns had to be evacuated as land had to be conceded to Russia. Twenty-five years after the move the displaced people tended to be richer. This was mostly because they were more mobile, in more urban regions and formally employed \cite{finland-ww2}.

\subsection{Do They Know?}
The question now is why aren't poorer people moving? Aren't they aware of the opportunity?

In Bangladesh a study \cite{monga-migration} offered rural people the transportation cost to the city during "hunger season". 22 percent took it and most found employment. They brought home far more than the cost of transportation or what they earned at home. But even so only half went back to the city the next year.

So despite knowing the facts people tend to \emph{not} move.

\subsection{Lift all the Boats?}
Is migration at the expense of the natives?

Plotting the wages of natives against the share of migrants you see an upward curving slope with more migrants. But this might be because migrants move to the best opportunities.

A study \cite{mariel-boatlift} around the Mariel boatlift looked at what happened when the Miami labour force increased by 7\% (mostly low skilled workers) due to a political decision in Cuba. Comparing with other US the wages of the natives were not affected. The study remains contended - most intensely by George Borjas.

There are a couple of similar studies and they all found "very little adverse impact on the local population".

\begin{mdframed}[style=mystyle,frametitle=US National Academy of Sciences]
Empirical research in recent decades suggests that findings remain by and large consistent with those in The New Americans National Research Council (1997) in that, when measured over a period of more than 10 years, the impact of immigration on the wages of natives overall is very small. \cite{immigration-consequences}
\end{mdframed}

\subsection{What's so Special about Immigrants?}
The supply-demand graph doesn't completely translate to migration because migrants also require work done. (E.g. they need a haircut, go to a restaurant, etc.)

If the "immigrating" workers are commuting back to their country to spend their money, this additional work requirement get's lost as an episode between Germany and Czech Republic showed.

Having more low-skilled workers slows down mechanization because these workers are cheaper. This was seen when California threw out Mexican field workers and mechanization was completely adopted within 3 years \cite{mechanization}.

Native low-skilled workers can change their occupations. They speak the local language while migrants don't. So they are higher skilled than the immigrants and shift to non-manual/more communication intensive jobs \cite{native-low-skilled}.

immigrants are willing to perform tasks natives are reluctant to carry out. This drives prices of such jobs as lawn mowing, babysitting etc. down and freeing natives' time to take on other jobs \cite{low-skilled-wages} - especially highly skilled women \cite{high-skilled-women}.

Because its hard to overcome the system of immigration control many immigrants bring exceptional talents that will help them become job creators. 43\% of the 500 largest companies were (co-)founded by immigrants \cite{immigrant-founders}.

\subsection{Workers and Watermelons}
Work is not an ordinary commodity. A work relationship lasts longer than the typical commodity (e.g. Watermelon). The quality of a worker is harder to judge than that of a commodity.

Relatively few companies hire those who just walk in and ask for a job. (Even if they accepted a lower wage.)

Because companies typically ask for references and referrals, established workers are quite secure from competition from newcomers.

Firms should pay more than the minimum the worker accepts because firing them wouldn't have the punitive effect (and also because of "They pretend to pay us, we pretend to work"). Economists call this the \keyword{efficiency wage}. And because this wage is not much less than what they pay the established workers the incentive to higher a migrant is low.

Migrants typically only force new native workers out of jobs - not established workers.

\subsection{The Skilled Set}
Highly skilled migrants (e.g. Doctors, Nurses) can lower the wages of the natives significantly. But at the same time services (Doctor's Appointment) get cheaper. This worsens the labor market prospects of the native workers with similar skills.

\subsection{What Caravan?}
Summary so far that people typically don't move unless there is a disaster pushing them.

\subsection{Without Connections}
People generally move to where they might know people.

If an employer is used to employees coming with recommendations he wouldn't hire one without. If all employers work like this an employee without recommendation is ruined because only the worst employees search jobs on the open market. This is called \keyword{adverse selection}.

\subsection{The Comforts of Home}
Lots of people in developing countries live in slums even though they could afford a bit more. But there are often several missing rungs in the quality ladder of housing. This is because only small parts of cities have decent-quality infrastructure and the land prices there are astronomical. India further limits building heights very harshly which makes cities larger and commutes longer.

The low-income migrant can then chose: Crowd into a slum, commute many hours a day from his home village, or sleep under a bridge or similar.

This combined with the prospect of working jobs nobody wants discourages everyone but those who can see past the initial obstacles and pain onto climbing the ladder for better times.

The poor often need the safety net of a community. If they move to the city they lose that. You only leave if you can afford the risk of leaving.

\subsection{Family Ties}
Families have many advantages to mitigate risk. But they also have disadvantages.

Enforcing your share in a family business might be financially bad for the whole family but better for you. (\$500 for 4 brothers - if you work you'll get paid \$100 - which is less than your share.)

Some parents under-invest in the education of their children to keep them close.

\subsection{Sleepless in Kathmandu}
Migrants typically overestimate the risks of migration as well as the prospects. \cite{nepal-misinfo} shows this for Nepal.

In general the confusion and misinformation can be stronger for either aspect and either boost or dampen migration.

\subsection{Risk versus Uncertainty}
Migrants often cannot quantify decisions they need to make which makes them hesitant.

\subsection{Through a Glass Darkly}
The status quo is the natural benchmark. If you migrate you could fail and be worse off than if you didn't. People hate this kind of personal mistakes. This concept is called \keyword{loss aversion}. (This is the reason why most people are over-insured.)

A would be migrant who stays home can always claim that he would have succeeded had he gone.

\subsection{After Tocqueville}
Until about the 1990s people moved around the US a lot - there was a lot of \keyword{restlessness}. But since then the labour market has become more segregated by skill level and lead to a sense of dislocation, with some regions left behind.

The reason for this segregation is that the wage gain from being in a booming city is lower for min6-class workers than for high-skilled workers \cite{wage-gap}. (E.g. a barista makes \$12 in Boston and \$9 in Boise but the living costs are much higher in Boston.) Therefore low to middle skill level workers have more money to use if they live outside the city.

If the economy turns and a worker looks for a new job he typically likes to look where he is currently because he has support from Family (e.g. grand-parents looking after kids). Sometimes trauma is added because people are fired from the only job they did in their life and need to re-start their life.

\subsection{The Comeback Cities Tour}
There were initiatives to bring work and workers back to the "heartland" of the US. But it's a chicken and egg problem: high educated workers will not come unless amenities exist but amenities can only thrive if enough workers live there. In the beginning for every open position a worker would have to move the city - they need convincing that it's worth the risk.

\subsection{Eisenhower and Stalin}
Mobility (internal and international) helps to even out standards of living. But forced mobility hurts the most vulnerable people. The fears of locals are typically about lower wages and cultural assimilation. When locals' fears get too large it typically yields stricter migration laws - until the foreign people are integrated and part of the locals.

We should help people willing to move instead of putting up obstacles. The best way is to ease integration.

\section{The Pains from Trade}

Economics agree that imposing tariffs is bad for the economy \cite{steel-tariffs}. But the public is not convinced \cite{people-like-economics}. They feel the USA is too open to trade.

The general proposition by economists is: Everyone is better off if everyone does what they do best.

\subsection{Stan Ulam's Challenge}\label{stolper-samuelson}
A country has a \keyword{Comparative Advantage} in industries they are \emph{relatively} good at producing. Trade doesn't work if only one party produces goods. They need something in return. Therefore every country will produce the goods it can make the best even if some other country could make it better. That means it would be worse at producing any other good. There is no way one participant will win in all markets. \cite{papers-samuelson} \& \cite{political-economy}

The next step is that countries abundant in labourers will have a comparative advantage in producing labour intensive goods. And vice versa capital abundant countries will produce capital intensive goods.\cite{protection-wages}

\subsection{Beauty is Truth, Truth Beauty}
When India opened to trade (in 1991) it had one bad year and then went back to GPD growth numbers of 6\% and finally to 7.5\% in the 2000s. It's not obvious whether it's a case for or against opening to trade.

\subsection{Whereof one cannot speak, Thereof one must be silent}
It is hard to figure out what was the reason for GPD growth. Was reducing tariffs pushing growths? Or was growth pushing away tariffs? How much is due to social changes?

Comparison with different countries is hard because they all have a huge set of changes in which they differ. Looking at all low to middle income countries the Stolper Samuelson logic from section \ref{stolper-samuelson} doesn't hold tight. Low skilled workers should be off better but typically are off worse. Much worse, inequality can be linked time-wise to trade liberalization.

Corellation is not causation. China opened up at about the same time. Possibly China was a competition for low skill workers.

\subsection{The Fact that could not be}
According to the Stolper Samuelson approach all workers in a country with the same skill have the same wage because they could just move.

Comparing districts exposure and poverty reduction during India's market liberalization in 1991 \cite{immobility-regional-impacts} shows that more exposure to market liberalization made poverty reduction slower. Subsequent papers found the same in Colombia and Brazil.

\subsection{The sticky Economy}
One important aspect is that districts most touched by liberalization were better off before but didn't improve as much. This reduced inequality across India.

This paper challenges the traditional trade theory's assumption that workers will move easily. Similarly, capital tends to be "sticky" because deciding bankers carry old decisions along in fear of being held accountable for new decisions. They will even give failing companies new loans to avoid/postpone their default.

Basically, everyone wants to preserve the status quo and workers and capital are slow to move companies and locations.

\subsection{Protection for Whom}
Often, regulations serve to keep imports out. But some of the regulations reflect consumer preferences (e.g. safety, environmental protections, worker conditions, ...). 

\subsection{What's in a Name}
Aid to Artisans (ATA) set out to set up a trade route from Egypt to richer markets. They built up an intermediary company that would find local producers and coordinate everything such that ATA could pull out again later. It took 1.5 years to secure the first larger order! Then business picked up.

Without ATA's name it would probably have been impossible to build the business because buying from a small foreign manufacturer is a gample. Handling issues takes a lot of time and time is money. Therefore there must be a big enough reduction in price to go through the hassle of an unknown supplier.

But the same holds true the other way around. An unknown buyer could change their mind and just return the merchandise. Young companies need to be able to absorb some losses due to their missing name.

\subsection{The World of Names}
For many items production cost is only 10-15\% of the price. This means that cutting production cost in half will only reduce the final price by at most 7.5\%. This makes it hard for new players to enter a market, especially without a reputation. A lower price isn't enough.

\subsection{The company you keep}
Caution against new companies can be self-fulfilling prophecies. If no one works with them they will vanish. If workers earned a reputation working in the West before returning home can give clients some peace of mind.

Single bad apples can spoil the trust in whole industries. Governments try to find ways to penalize bad quality producers. The best case scenario is that an industry cluster can share reputation by being in the same location or collaborate to fulfill a large order by each providing a part of the work.

This model might be changing though because Amazon and Alibaba take the place of the middle man and connect producers and buyers directly.

\subsection{Was it worth \$2.4 trillion?}
An easy way to pomote exports is to manipulate the currency to be weak. Selling at the same dollar price will give you more local money. But imports are more expensive.

China did this through the 2000s because it tends to buy less than it sells which would otherwise drive up their currency.

\subsection{The China shock}
A study \cite{china-syndrome} of job losses in the US during China's ascent as an export nation shows that areas producing goods that China now produces were hit harder than those producing other products. They most often lost more jobs than just the production jobs. This is probably because fired workers had to tighten their belt which created ripple effects. Nonmanufacturing employment couldn't absorb the unemployed showing that workers don't move their location or industry.

\subsection{Clusterf**k}
If an area has a concentration on a certain industry the whole region depends on it being stable. If the industry declines the whole area declines.

\subsection{Forget the losers}
The study \cite{china-syndrome} also looked at government support in affected areas. The wages there went down by \$549 and welfare went up by only \$58 per person. Only \$0.23 was to help people adjust to new jobs and places. Most of the welfare increase was due to disability insurance. This is typically a final way out of the working market. Some also needed to apply for disability to get healthcare.

But setting up a good program to support the losers of trade is hard - especially the political part. 

\subsection{Is trade worth it?}
For the USA, trade isn't a big part of the economy. They import only about 8\% of spending.

\subsection{Computing the gains from trade: A slightly technical aside}
In a study \cite{predictions-of-trade} imports are consolidated into one product and analyzed how sensitive imports are to trade costs. This allows to estimate gains from trade at about 1\% GDP for easily substitutable goods and 4\% for difficult to substitute goods. 

\subsection{Size matters}
The USA are large enough to produce everything themselves. But trade is much more important to smaller countries like Belgium with an import share above 30\%. These countries need trade.

For larger developing countries the biggest problem is often internal integration: missing infrastructure like roads and trains. This hinders them from producing everything themselves. 

\subsection{Is small beautiful}
Small firms cannot specialize workers and are therefore less productive then larger ones. Similarly, farmers in integrated countries can specialize in an efficient crop for their fields instead of having to grow everything. And poor integration/transport can erode cost advantages of a cheap labor force. 

\subsection{Don't start that trade war}
While tariffs can support one industry, it will most likely shift the pain to other industries that are currently relying on trade because of retaliatory tariffs. For the USA-China trade war that would typically be agriculture. Therefore a loss of job in agriculture would be the first effect.

As we saw, there is a big inequality of winners and losers and it's hard to redistribute these gains to the losers because transitioning workers is hard. 

\subsection{If not tariffs, what? Ease mobility, accept immobility}
There will be losers from trade as well as tariffs. The government should help them to be mobile both in location as well as industry so they can move to unaffected sectors. If they cannot be mobile, there must be some way of compensation like supporting companies that employ older workers. But such proposals face political difficulties.


\section{Likes, Wants, and Needs}
Economists distinguish between preferences and beliefs: The distinction is that for a preference you have all information while there is missing information for beliefs. This is important because we need to distinguish between what individuals need and what they want.

Theory says that it's good business practice to be tolerant so you can do business with everyone. But bigotry can be good business if you have bigots as customers.

\subsection{De gustibus non est disuptandum}
In \cite{de-gustibus} they argue that economics should not try to understand preferences. We overrule people's choices as bad (e.g. giving food stamps rather than cash). But generally their choices make sense (e.g. a bored starving man might buy a TV rather than food to stop the bore-out). \cite{de-gustibus} assumes preferences to be stable because it scientifically makes more sense to investigate why a choice might make sense rather than assume it is irrational.

Random moves can influence a whole decision chain in different people (e.g. people crowding around the first person on a train platform assuming they know where the doors are going to be). A signal from others can alter our beliefs/behaviors. But that doesn't explain why people do things they know to go against their interests (e.g. getting an ugly tattoo).

\subsection{Collective Action}
Social norms and adhering to them can be rationalized as well. The basic idea is that those who violate the norm will be punished by the rest of the community which deters violations. \cite{folk-theorem1} \cite{folk-theorem2}

In many developing countries some part of the land is always held as common property to help those with ill fate can get back up using that land. Systems of mutual help like this can break when too many members have external opportunities and don't fully participate in them anymore.

\subsection{Collective Reaction}
Norms can also be bad. It can also be shown that such norms sustain e.g. the Indian caste system. \cite{social-norms-behaviors-growth}

\subsection{The Doctor and the Saint}
There is a tension between a community that's binding and one that's bullying. Similarly there is a tension between a state that protects the individual and one that undermines the community.

There are examples of cases where the individual needs protection and the country even writes law to enforce that protection but the community just overrides it with its own rules because the majority is better off that way.

\subsection{``Black guy asks Nation for Change''}
The USA still has a big problem that the African American population has a 30 percent wage gap \cite{wage-gap-pewresearch}. This is because African Americans get incarcerated more often than anyone else \cite{incarceration-gap}.

The title (from the Onion) plays on a black guy (Obama) wanting some change (coins) vs. wanting to change the nation.

\subsection{This time it's different}
Resentment of immigrants in the US is much bigger in states with almost no immigrants. Therefore, it's more identity then economical resentment. They don't know the immigrants and imagine them fundamentally different. The election of Trump also made it easier to talk about such resentments.

This isn't new to the US. Benjamin Franklin hated the Germans. Later there were immigration quotas introduced for Eastern and Suthern Europeans (Italians and Greeks). But foreigners assimilated and by now pizza is one of the five basic food groups.

Every wave of immigration triggers a "this time it's different" reaction at first until the people are accepted later on.

\subsection{Statistical Discrimination}
There are many reasons for bigoted behavior towards other groups. They might be better off in your city - violence then is hiding theft. Or people feel they need to be intolerant in order to belong to a group or keep valubale social connections.

Statictical discrimination is about the race being correlated with some (negative) characteristics like drug dealing and crime. E.g. employers could have a box on the application form that needs to be checked if you have a criminal conviction. Young black men are more likely to have such a conviction and their unemployment rate is double the national average.

A study randomized the convictions on fictuous applications between races and found that removing the checkbox hurt the black population. Before white applicants received 7\% more callbacks while after they received 43\%. The problem is that now employers just assume that black applicants have convictions. (They identify them by typical names and other biographical markers.)

A different study (Trust Game) builds on one person being given money. They can then send any amount to some other person. The amount sent is trippled by the study. The other person can then share back as much as they want. Mostly more than half is shared in the first step and more comes back in the second step than was originally sent. The amount sent is lower though when the other party is typically discriminated against. But if there is no channel back to the original sender, about the same amount is sent to both groups. Therefore the difference seems to be suspicion/mistrust rather than animosity. (Sadly no citations here.)

\subsection{Self-Reinforcing Discrimination}
In a study, when told a test is a loratory problem-solving task, black and white students performed about the same. But when told it's a test for "intellectual ability" black students performed lower than whites. \cite{stereotype-performance} These kind of tests have been done with gender self-discrimination as well as majority self-discrimination (e.g. "a test to see why asians outperform white people in math").

Similarly, self-fulfilling profecies can be created e.g. when a teacher is told certain students were gifted. These students then develop better than the rest of the class.

Prejudices can also lead to similar effects. If women are seen as less able, men will listen less and learn less from them even though they might be better teachers. Or if they are line managers, line workers might be unhappy because of prejudice, do inferior work to the other workers and thereby making women the worse line managers through no fault of their own. \cite{women-line-managers}

\subsection{Can African Americans Play Golf}
When primed about race, people tend to perform the way they perceive their race to perform. E.g. if a test is framed as "natural sports ability", African Americans will perform better while white Americans will perform better on a test framed as "sports intelligence".

\subsection{Acting White}
There are studies that show hispanic students trying to avoid acting white - like having scholarly aspirations. Otherwise they might be singled out by their peers.

\subsection{Let's try to Account for Tastes}
There is a study where bankers were asked to flip a coin 20 times. If they flipped heads more often they would get a compensation while tails would not. When asked questions reminding them of their banker role before, they would cheat more than when asked about their favorite leisure activity reminding them of being a regular person. \cite{banker-dishonesty} 

\subsection[Motivated Beliefs]{Motivated Beliefs \cite{motivated-beliefs}}
Our beliefs are shaped by our emotional needs e.g. because we feel terrible when we disappoint ourselves. We might even distort our beliefs about others to fit our needs e.g. to validate our behaviors like not pushing African cashiers as much as white cashiers because "they wouldn't respond anyway".

This is then amlified by us adopting any information that is supporting our case and because we have "good arguments" anyone who doesn't agree might be attacking our intelligence. This obviously leads to conflict.
That also explains why people don't react well to being fact-checked. The reaction is more of a "how dare you challenge my beliefs." Only after some processing might they actually change their beliefs.

Reminding people that they are good and should adhere to their good ways is more effective than to tell them that they should be good and adhere to good ways. The problem begins when the self-esteem gets lower like that of white populations in the rust belt. If they now live on welfare, the perceived "worse off" latino mother can't be a welfare queen anymore - she needs to be a gang member.

To break this circle we need to rethink the social security net to give people back positive self-value.

\subsection{Coherent Arbitrariness}
Two experiments show that people don't put a lot of thought into how they value things they own or buy and can be influenced relatively easily to change their value estimates.

\subsection{Robbers Cave}
It is relatively easy to influence people to build animosity towards other groups or to work together with them instead. Just giving a different name to a random group leads to members favoring their group. But if there are obstacles people just as easily reunite.

E.g. the Tutsis vs. Hutus animosity was originally stirred by the Belgian colonialists. Before they were a mostly homogeneous people. 

\subsection{Homophily}
People tend to be looking and associating with people who are similar. This happens mostly because they share the same set of values and views. But this also leads to segregation.

\subsection{Echo Chambers and Holograms}
This segregation can lead us to be exposed only to the same opinions prevalent in our group even for factual thigns (e.g. on climate change or genetically engineered food).

These echo chambers can be (ab-)used by companies and people. E.g. politicians are currently helping to divide people into groups that even adopt their language. (You can identify which political party a politician leans towards by checking which words he uses.)

\subsection{The new Public Space?}
While there always was separation and people tended to have more friends within their party lines, the cosumption of news within party lines and the separation of the parties positions has increased significantly. This also brought the news outlets to tweak their programs towards more specific user groups.

Interestingly though, this separation happened more for people 65 and older than for young people (18-39).

\subsection{Networking not Working}
One aspect of the separation is that it's easy to produce and distribute fake news that cater to your audience. Real news are much more expensive to produce. Real content is often only copy pasted without naming or paying the source which makes real news even less attractive to produce.

At the same time echo chambers repeat the same ideas endlessly leading to people to believe them just by the mass of reports.

Communication on the internet is more direct and often abbreviated (e.g. on Twitter) which erodes the norms of civic discourse. Twitter also makes it easy to pitch wild claims and see if they stick.

Finally, today's social media platforms automate what content you see. So you can't really compensate for bias in the sources anymore. (When actively chosing the sources, users tend to correct for this bias \cite{known-source-slant}).

\subsection{Running Together}
With (political) groups getting more and more separated, voting becomes more and more a cencus of these groups rather than a balancing act of priorities. Because the winner doesn't have to deliver on his promises and supporters worry about a takeover of the other side, leaders can abuse their power to suppress alternative voices/media.

Only a social policy founded on respect for the dignity of the individual opens them to tolerance towards others. The \emph{contact hypothesis} \cite{contact-hypothesis} stipulates that interpersonal contact is one of the most effective ways to reduce prejudice (under appropriate conditions).

One key place this should happen is in schools and universities when people are young and still more plastic. Studies in the US \cite{us-contact-hypothesis} and India \cite{india-contact-hypothesis} show that this can work.

\subsection{Students for fair Admission vs. Harvard}
While the mixing of races is one of the most powerful instruments for tolerance, affirmative action is now a polarizing idea in itself. Both, that Latino and African Americans are supported and Asian Americans are hindered is increasingly contested as unfair. In India caste quotas in educational institutions and government jobs are similarly resented and quotas amended with eligibility criteria like income.

The core discussion is between technical merit (which we saw can be influenced by many factors like self-discrimination) and personality ratings. A massive overrepresentation of any social group relative to its weight in the population is undesirable in a democracy and will likely lead to political problems.

The main problem with these acceptance criteria is that it stirs resentments between the groups. This in itself can cause the contact hypothesis to break down because puplis enter the situation not as equals but with resentments. 

\subsection{Cricket Lessions}
This was proven by an Indian study \cite{cricket-study} that found that mixed caste cricket teams helped making friends across caste lines. But it also showed that caste based teams that played other castes were less likely to make friends across caste lines than the ones that only played against the same caste (or not at all): Competition undermines contact and it's important to have a shared goal.

Similarly the World Cup wins of France in 1998 and 2018 brought together the nation and showed that people from the banlieus aren't just lazy bums.

\subsection{Zoning for Peace}
An alternative way to incorporate the contact hypothesis are mixed neighborhoods. But they prove to be notoriously unstable. There is a tipping point when a fraction of relative population (typically blacks) leads others to leave a neighborhood. This then brings about a cycle that leads to most of the second group leaving because the have less and less representation.

This fraction changes from city to city with Chicaco only tolerating 5\% but mostly being between 12\% and 15\% \cite{tipping-point}. Therefore, a good strategy would be to disperse public housing throughout the city such that no "pure" neighborhoods can arise. Singapore even goes so far that it ensures strict quotas in every block. The difficulty here is mostly political and twofold. Firstly, the will to enforce this. But secondly, public housing in fancy neighborhoods can be abused as patronage.

Another approach is to shuttle kids to schools outside their neighborhoods to foster diversity. But this is unpopular. Not least because kids don't like to be shuttled larger distances. But lower income children could be allowed to attend schools outside their neighborhoods voluntarily - which showed to be beneficial.

\subsection{Rearranging the Deck Chairs?}
The sum of these proposals might seem modest but since the prejudice is not only symptom but also cause of the malaise small corrections might be enough.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Reseinting those with racist sentiments only reinforces those sentiments because then they don't feel respected.
	\item Prejudice is not an absolute preference. If they can be focussed on other things they can be won.
	\item Voters may not feel passionate about their racist views. They just vote for the guy who looks and sounds like them.
	\item The most effective way to combat prejudice is not to combat it but to engage with other policy issues. On these we then must deliver such that it soothes the anger and deprivation so many feel.
\end{enumerate}
